## 1. Summary of the record

> this summary retells rows that the headings below retell in full; it adds nothing to them. Counts from the database state what the database lists, one researcher's coding of court decisions, never what happened in each court. The two state supreme court decisions were read whole, from copies the database hosts.

The AI Hallucination Cases Database is compiled by Damien Charlotin `[F022]`. He says it
covers decisions where AI use, "whether established or merely alleged", is
addressed by a court or tribunal, and only cases where the court found or
implied that a party relied on hallucinated content `[F022]`. As downloaded on
10 October 2026, it lists 2,185 decisions, dated from 14 April 2023 to 8 October
2026 `[F027]`.

The database lists "Pro Se Litigant" in its party column in 1,259 entries and
"Lawyer" in 865 `[F028]`. It names a specific tool in 234 entries, about one in
nine `[F030]` `[F035]`, and its page warns that naming a tool does not necessarily
mean that tool was responsible `[F039]`.

In Nebraska, the lawyer
said he did not rely on AI `[F004]`, and the court did not decide whether AI was
used: it said its analysis was the same either way `[F008]`. It struck the
brief, dismissed the appeal and referred the lawyer for discipline `[F011]`. In
New Mexico, the lawyer acknowledged using ChatGPT `[F014]`, and the court held
him in direct contempt `[F019]` and ordered him to pay $5,000 `[F020]`.

## 3. The system and its configuration

There is no single system here: the database records decisions about many
filers and many tools `[F028]` `[F031]`. It names ChatGPT as the only tool in 114 entries and in
136 in all, more than any other product `[F031]`. It says it covers decisions
where AI use is established or merely alleged `[F022]`, and that it does not
track the wider universe of fake citations or AI use in court filings
`[F024]`. It lists 1,492 decisions from the United States alone, and one more
alongside another country `[F033]`.

## 4. Conditions before the event

> the captured documents say little about conditions before the decisions; this heading records what they do say.

The oldest decision the database lists is dated 14 April 2023 `[F027]`. The
Nebraska Supreme Court described the case as involving "fictitious,
potentially AI-generated" cases, holdings and quotations `[F008]`.

## 5. Sequence of events

- **20 March 2026.** The Nebraska Supreme Court files its opinion in Prososki v.
  Regan `[F001]`, having moved the appeal to its own docket to address numerous
  nonexistent or inaccurate citations and quotations in the appellant's brief
  `[F002]`.
- **Before 21 August 2026.** The Supreme Court of New Mexico, on its own motion,
  orders the appellant's lawyer in State v. Sandoval to show cause why he should
  not be held in contempt `[F012]`.
- **21 August 2026.** The lawyer appears and argues before that court `[F013]`.
  The court's order states no filing date; the only date it gives is the
  hearing `[F040]`.
- **8 October 2026.** The newest decision the database lists `[F027]`.

## 6. How the facts were established

**In Nebraska, the court did not establish the cause.** It found that the
"Kennedy v. Kennedy" authority in the brief was fictitious, borrowing the name
of a real 2019 unpublished opinion that does not match the citation `[F006]`. It
said the lawyer's Westlaw explanation could account for a handful of citations
but not the fictitious quotations or the cases that appear made up entirely,
and it found his wrong-draft explanation lacked credibility `[F007]`. The lawyer
conceded at oral argument, only after questioning from the bench, that the
authority was not properly grounded in Nebraska law `[F009]`. Whether AI was
used, the court did not decide `[F008]`.

**In New Mexico, the court records the lawyer's own admissions.** The court records
that he acknowledged using ChatGPT `[F014]`, admitted the brief contained false
testimony from wholly fabricated witnesses and misrepresented legal authority in
two named cases `[F015]`, and admitted he did not verify the brief before
signing and filing it `[F016]`, and admitted he did not tell his client about the
show-cause proceedings `[F017]`. The court concluded that he showed a lack of
remorse and of concern for his client `[F018]`.

**In the database, a specific tool is mostly not named.** It names a specific
tool in 234 of its 2,185 entries; 1,470 say "Implied" and 455 "Unidentified"
`[F030]`. It marks 22 entries "Alleged: Yes" and does not define the term
further `[F034]`; the compiler calls including some alleged cases a judgment
call on his part `[F023]`.

## 7. Detection and response

Nebraska held that
fictitious authority, "whether through generative AI or not", can be resolved
under existing rules of professional conduct and court rules `[F010]`. New
Mexico acted on its own motion `[F012]`.

## 8. Outcomes and measured effects

- **Nebraska:** the brief struck, the appeal dismissed, and the lawyer referred to
  the Counsel for Discipline `[F011]`.
- **New Mexico:** with five justices concurring, the lawyer found in direct
  contempt, referred to the Disciplinary Board, barred from appearing before the
  court pending that outcome, all briefing struck, and the Public Defender
  appointed for his client `[F019]`; he was ordered to pay $5,000 to the State
  Bar's Client Protection Fund `[F020]`.
- **Across the database:** a professional sanction is recorded in 164 entries and
  a monetary penalty in 380 `[F032]`.

> the penalties are counted here, not summed.

## 9. Statements by the parties

The Nebraska Supreme Court records that the lawyer told it the citations were
"sloppy" from "copying and pasting . . . from Westlaw without verifying the
official reporter" `[F003]`; that he did not invent cases, did not rely on AI,
and did not attempt to mislead the court `[F004]`; and that he attributed the
fictitious material to a screen crack on his laptop and to filing the wrong
draft `[F005]`. These are the lawyer's account as the court recorded it; the
court found the wrong-draft explanation not credible `[F007]`.

## 10. Discrepancies in the record

> each discrepancy is recorded here, not resolved.

- **The database's own count.** Its page states 2,184 cases and "Lawyer (864)";
  the file downloaded the same evening lists 2,185 entries, 865 of them listing
  "Lawyer" `[F036]` `[F025]`.
- **The database's scope, described two ways.** Its main sentence speaks of cases
  "where generative AI produced hallucinated content", while its note covers
  decisions where AI use is established or merely alleged `[F038]`.

- **The database's coding against the Nebraska decision.** The database lists
  the case with "Lawyer" under "Party Using AI", "Unidentified" as the tool and
  "Alleged: No", although the court did not decide whether AI was used `[F041]`.

## 11. What the record does not establish

- Whether AI produced the fictitious authority in the Nebraska brief `[F008]`.
- Which tool, if any, lies behind most database entries `[F030]`, and whether a
  named tool was responsible `[F039]`.
- How many fake citations or uses of AI in court filings the database does not
  capture: the compiler says it does not track that wider universe `[F024]`.
- The New Mexico order is non-precedential: it records what the court ordered,
  not a rule for other cases `[F021]`.
