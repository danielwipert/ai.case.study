## 1. Summary of the record

> Scope note, not a finding: this summary retells a few rows that the headings below retell in full; it adds nothing to them. The discrimination claims in the lawsuit are untried: the Record states what each party alleges or says, never whether it is true.

> Unless marked otherwise, statements below come from D01, the court's discovery order of 28 May 2026 in Mobley v. Workday.

On 28 May 2026, United States Magistrate Judge Laurel Beeler signed a
discovery order in Mobley v. Workday, Inc., filed on 29 May 2026 as document
340 `[F001]`. The order describes the case as one in which the plaintiffs are
suing Workday, Inc., "for utilizing an AI screening system that is more likely
to deny applicants who are African American, suffer from disabilities, or are
over forty years old." `[F018]`

The order denied the plaintiffs' motion to compel Workday to produce its
bias-testing data and its customers' applicant data `[F003]`. The order states
that attorney-client privilege applies to Workday's bias-testing data because
Workday's attorneys curated the underlying data and used the results in
providing legal advice `[F006]`. The order states that it addresses only the
bias-testing data over which Workday claims privilege `[F011]`. On the
customers' data, the order states that on the record before it the court could
not compel production, and that the parties should be able to work together to
resolve the issue `[F013]`.

The order also directed Workday to produce its EEO-1 and OFCCP documents
`[F004]`, stating that Workday uses the same AI tools as its customers and that
those documents are therefore relevant to its knowledge of potential
demographic disparities when using AI tools `[F015]`.

The documents disagree about the data behind Workday's 2026 bias analysis of
its Spotlight tool: Workday's page says it used a sample of Workday's own applicant flow data, and the plaintiffs' motion calls the data behind all three external
audits "10 Global Large Customers data" `[F045]` `[F046]`.

## 3. The system and its configuration

The court's order of 28 May 2026 states that customers who buy Workday
Recruiting have access to Candidate Skills Match, an algorithmic feature that
determines how far an applicant's skills match the role applied for `[F016]`.
The order states that in 2023 Workday engaged an external consultant to test
its product Spotlight using the impact-ratio approach described in New York
City Local Law 144 `[F017]`.

Workday's page "HiredScore's Focus on Responsible AI and Bias Mitigation", as
archived on 5 September 2025, states that the Spotlight functionality was not
designed as nor intended to be an automated employment decision tool as
defined under New York City's law `[F042]`. Workday's page "Responsible AI and
Bias Mitigation for HiredScore Spotlight", as captured on 2 October 2026,
states that it does not suggest that Spotlight is or is not an automated
employment decision tool under any particular law `[F044]`.

## 4. Conditions before the event

> Scope note, not a finding: this heading retells what the documents say about Workday's bias testing and the discovery dispute before the order. The first part retells Workday's own pages; the second is the plaintiffs' account in their motion of 20 April 2026 (D02), retold as theirs. Workday's own filings on privilege were not captured (heading 12).

> Workday's published bias tests.

Workday's page "HiredScore's Focus on
Responsible AI and Bias Mitigation", as archived by the Internet Archive on 5
September 2025, states that Workday commissioned an external auditor to test
the Spotlight AI using the approach and formulas in New York City's law for
its "bias audits", and that the purpose of the testing furthers Workday and
HiredScore's commitments to identify and mitigate potential bias `[F040]`. The
same page reports that the auditor's test, completed on 21 September 2024,
used "a random sample of 10 Global Large Customers data over the last 12
months of reqs and candidates, covering requisitions that were opened and/or
filled between Sep 2023 to Sep 2024", and found "no evidence of disparate
impact" on the calculated impact ratios `[F041]`.

Workday's page "Responsible AI and Bias Mitigation for HiredScore Spotlight",
as captured on 2 October 2026, reports that Secretariat, which Workday calls
an independent third party, analysed a sample of Workday's own applicant flow
data from its deployment of Spotlight, completed the analysis on 20 March
2026, and found "no evidence of disparate impact"; what data the analysis used
is disputed (heading 10) `[F043]` `[F045]` `[F046]`. The page reports
that the analysis covered applications from U.S. residents for New York
City-area positions from 1 September 2025 to 28 February 2026, in the five
highest-volume job profiles, keeping only profiles with enough applicants in
each group and excluding applicants who gave no or conflicting gender or race
information `[F043]`. The page says the results are specific to Workday's
implementation of Spotlight and that outcomes may differ in other settings
`[F043]`.

> The discovery dispute, as the plaintiffs tell it.

The plaintiffs' motion
to compel of 20 April 2026 states that Workday's external auditor conducted
bias audits in July 2023, in September 2024, and on 20 March 2026 for the
period 1 September 2025 to 28 February 2026, and that Workday had not produced
the data underlying those audits `[F026]`. The motion states that Workday's
third privilege log lists bias testing activities as early as July 2019
`[F025]`. The motion says that Workday's AI Fact Sheet, a discovery document,
describes bias testing as a standard component of Workday's "ML Trust
Program", its "lifecycle review process" and its product development
obligations under NYC Local Law 144 and OFCCP guidance `[F024]`.

The motion states that Workday produced privilege logs in January 2025, on 13
March 2025 and on 31 December 2025; that none of those three logs identified
privilege log entries 488, 489 or 490 or disclosed the withholding of Python
bias testing code, JSON data files or bias testing results "in the formats now
claimed as privileged"; and that Workday first asserted privilege over those
three categories on 6 April 2026, in a fourth privilege log `[F023]`. The
motion states that Workday's response to the court's 6 April 2026 production
deadline was a single sentence from counsel: "We are also confirming our
document production is complete." `[F028]` The motion states that class
certification briefing begins in July 2026 and that the plaintiffs'
statistical expert needs the bias testing data, code and results for the
analysis behind the certification motion `[F022]`.

## 8. Outcomes and measured effects

> Scope note, not a finding: this heading retells what the order decided and the reasons it gives. A discovery order decides what must be handed over; it does not decide whether Workday's tools discriminate.

> What the order decided.

The court's order of 28 May 2026 denied the
plaintiffs' motion to compel Workday to produce its bias-testing data and its
customers' applicant data `[F003]`. It ordered Workday to produce its EEO-1
and OFCCP documents `[F004]`. It terminated as moot the letter brief on
deanonymizing applicant data, stating that "[t]he plaintiffs have admitted that they do not need
applicant names" `[F005]`.

> The order's reasons on the bias-testing data.

The order states that
attorney-client privilege applies to Workday's bias-testing data because
Workday's attorneys curated the underlying data and used the results in
providing legal advice `[F006]`. It states that Workday's invoking the mere
existence of its bias testing outside of litigation is not enough to waive
privilege `[F010]`. It states that the plaintiffs had not shown that
disclosing the HiredScore testing data to auditors waived privilege over all
of Workday's bias-testing data, and that the order addresses only the
bias-testing data over which Workday claims privilege `[F011]`. It states that
Workday's argument that its bias-testing data was not relevant was
unpersuasive, and that even if the data is not the best evidence of disparate
impact it has at least some probative value `[F012]`.

> The order's reasons on the other data.

On Workday's customers' data, the
order states that, on the record before it, the court could not compel
production, and that given some third parties' apparent position that Workday
is the better source, the parties should be able to work together to resolve
the issue `[F013]`. On the EEO-1 and OFCCP documents, the order states that
Workday uses the same AI tools as its customers, and that those documents are
therefore relevant to Workday's knowledge of potential demographic disparities
when using AI tools `[F015]`.

## 9. Statements by the parties

> Scope note, not a finding: each statement is retold as the party's own, not as fact. The plaintiffs' description of the audits' data is retold under heading 10.

> The plaintiffs.

The court's order reports the plaintiffs' argument that
Workday waived privilege by relying on an "AI Fact Sheet" which states that
Workday performs bias testing `[F009]`, and their reply that they had
subpoenaed thirty-five third parties and that at least some of them objected
that the plaintiffs should seek production from Workday `[F014]`.

The plaintiffs' motion of 20 April 2026 argues that Workday's bias-testing
materials are technical and statistical artifacts, not legal communications,
and that neither attorney-client privilege nor the work product doctrine
protects them `[F020]`. The motion argues that any work-product protection was
waived when Workday publicly disclosed the existence and conclusions of its
bias testing program through its AI Fact Sheet, its public website, external
auditors and executives' public statements `[F021]`. The motion states that
Workday at first denied that the external bias audits existed and amended its
response on 19 December 2025, after the plaintiffs had found the audits
themselves `[F027]`. The motion states that Workday concedes it possesses and
hosts the customer applicant data at issue `[F029]`.

In the discovery letter of 7 May 2026, the plaintiffs state that Workday
produced only anonymized data on people who applied for jobs at Workday, with
the names removed, which prevents each opt-in plaintiff from identifying which
of the 216,622 anonymized candidate IDs is theirs `[F031]`. The plaintiffs
state that the applicant data Workday produced in March 2026 was found to be
corrupted and incomplete, and that its replacement, delivered on 15 April
2026, shows similar deficiencies `[F037]`. The plaintiffs argue that the
applicant data Workday produced cannot be taken at face value, and, citing
Workday's opposition brief (ECF No. 315 at 14), write that Workday "admits"
it "reflect[s] Workday's counsel's strategic curation of data to be tested."
`[F036]` 

> Scope note, not a finding: which data Workday's own words in that brief described is not established from the documents read; the order cites the same page for a different dispute. They are not retold here as Workday's position on privilege.

> Workday.

In Workday's section of the discovery letter of 7 May 2026,
Workday states that the plaintiffs seek the names of more than 215,000
applicants to Workday, that at least 200,000 of them are not parties to the
case, and that there are only 15,000 opt-in plaintiffs `[F033]`. Workday
states that it has repeatedly offered to de-anonymize data for any specific
opt-in plaintiff, and that the plaintiffs refuse to provide the identifying
information needed to do so `[F034]`. Workday states that it does not contend
it is unable to link applicant names to application records, and that it
decided not to produce name-identified data to protect the privacy of
non-party applicants `[F035]`.

Workday's Form 10-Q for the quarter ended 31 July 2026, dated 27 August 2026,
states: "We are currently defending against a lawsuit alleging that certain of
our AI-related products and services enable discrimination, and although we
believe that such claims lack merit, and the majority of the claims have been
dismissed, legal proceedings can be lengthy, expensive, and disruptive to our
operations and customers" `[F038]`. On the library's reading, as of 2 October 2026, the 10-Q does not name the
lawsuit (heading 11) `[F039]`.
